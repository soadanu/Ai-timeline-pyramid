## 1. Mythical and mechanical roots of thinking machines (Before 1950)

**What happened:** Long before computers, myths such as the Greek bronze guardian Talos and later clockwork automata imagined artificial beings, while philosophers tried to describe reasoning as a set of rules.

**Why it matters:** These ideas posed the central question of the field: if reasoning can be described precisely, can a machine carry it out?

**Subfield:** Foundations

**Impact:** In daily life they still shape how books, films and news picture AI. In industry and policy they colour debates about how human-like machines should be allowed to look, sound and behave.

*Note on accuracy: This is a span of centuries, not a single date, so it sits at the very base of the pyramid.*

## 2. Turing proposes the Turing Test (1950)

**What happened:** Alan Turing published 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence' and proposed the imitation game, now called the Turing Test: if a person cannot reliably tell a machine's written answers from a human's, the machine can be said to behave intelligently.

**Why it matters:** It turned 'can machines think?' into a question that could be tested, and it gave the field a practical yardstick based on behaviour rather than philosophy.

**Subfield:** Foundations

**Impact:** The idea still frames how people judge chatbots and assistants. It also lives on in everyday web security: CAPTCHA, the puzzle that asks you to prove you are human, is named after the test.

## 3. Dartmouth workshop names 'artificial intelligence' (1956)

**What happened:** In the summer of 1956 researchers including John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon met at Dartmouth College for a workshop on making machines simulate learning and intelligence. McCarthy introduced the term 'artificial intelligence' in the proposal for it.

**Why it matters:** A shared name and a shared agenda turned scattered experiments into one research field, and the group's ideas set the research agenda for decades.

**Subfield:** Foundations

**Impact:** The labs, university courses and funding programmes that grew from it produced the technology behind search engines, voice assistants and automation. Today 'AI' is a standard budget line in almost every large company.

*Note on accuracy: Sources differ slightly: the term appears in the 1955 proposal, and the workshop itself ran in summer 1956. Check your course lesson for the wording it uses.*

## 4. The Logic Theorist proves mathematical theorems (1956)

**What happened:** Allen Newell, Herbert Simon and Cliff Shaw built the Logic Theorist, which proved 38 of the first 52 theorems in Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica and found a more elegant proof for one of them than the published version.

**Why it matters:** It demonstrated symbolic reasoning: manipulating symbols and rules to solve problems, which became the dominant approach to AI for the next three decades.

**Subfield:** Symbolic AI

**Impact:** Its descendants are automated theorem provers and formal verification tools, used by chip makers and software companies to prove that hardware and critical code behave correctly.

## 5. ELIZA, the first chatbot (1966)

**What happened:** Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT created ELIZA. Its best-known script, DOCTOR, imitated a psychotherapist by spotting keywords and turning the user's own words back as questions.

**Why it matters:** It showed that conversation could be simulated with very simple rules, and that people project understanding onto software. Weizenbaum was alarmed by how attached some users became, and this 'ELIZA effect' is still studied.

**Subfield:** Natural Language Processing

**Impact:** ELIZA is the ancestor of customer-service chatbots and virtual assistants. Its lesson, that fluent language is not the same as understanding, matters more than ever with today's convincing chatbots.

## 6. Expert systems go commercial (1980s)

**What happened:** Expert systems captured the know-how of human specialists as if-then rules. The best-known, R1 (also called XCON), configured computer orders for Digital Equipment Corporation and reportedly saved the company about $25 million a year by 1982.

**Why it matters:** They proved AI could deliver business value, which triggered the first commercial AI boom. Their weaknesses, costly knowledge gathering and brittleness outside their narrow domain, helped lead to the next AI winter.

**Subfield:** Symbolic AI

**Impact:** Rule engines descended from expert systems still run decisions in insurance underwriting, tax software, loan approval and manufacturing configuration.

*Note on accuracy: The $25 million figure is a reported estimate from the course material, so describe it as 'reportedly'.*

## 7. Backpropagation makes multi-layer networks trainable (1986)

**What happened:** David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams published 'Learning representations by back-propagating errors' in Nature. It popularised backpropagation, a method that passes errors backwards through a network so every layer's weights can be adjusted.

**Why it matters:** It solved the training problem that had stalled neural networks after the perceptron's limits were exposed, and it is still the core training method of deep learning.

**Subfield:** Neural Networks

**Impact:** Early applications, such as Yann LeCun's handwritten-digit networks, were used in the 1990s to read postal codes and bank cheques automatically, an early sign of machine learning in everyday services.

## 8. The AI winters (1980s–1990s)

**What happened:** Twice, enthusiasm and funding for AI collapsed after early promises went unmet: first in the mid-1970s, and again from the late 1980s into the early 1990s, when the market for expert systems and specialised AI hardware crashed.

**Why it matters:** The winters forced researchers toward measurable, data-driven methods and more realistic claims, setting the stage for machine learning to take over.

**Subfield:** Field History

**Impact:** They remain the standard warning for investors, companies and governments about overpromising. Debates today about an 'AI bubble' echo the same pattern.

*Note on accuracy: Dates are fuzzy because the winters were gradual. The brief groups them as 1980s–1990s, and many sources also describe an earlier winter in the 1970s.*

## 9. Deep Blue defeats Kasparov (1997)

**What happened:** In May 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat world chess champion Garry Kasparov 3.5 to 2.5 in a six-game rematch, searching around 200 million positions per second using custom hardware and hand-tuned evaluation rules.

**Why it matters:** It showed that fast search plus expert knowledge could beat the best human at a game long seen as a mark of intelligence. It did this by brute force rather than by learning.

**Subfield:** Game AI / Search

**Impact:** The match changed public attitudes to what machines could do. The search and planning ideas behind it appear in route planning, scheduling and logistics software.

*Note on accuracy: Kasparov won an earlier 1996 match against Deep Blue. The famous result is from the 1997 rematch.*

## 10. DARPA challenges drive autonomous vehicles (2004–2007)

**What happened:** DARPA's 2004 Grand Challenge sent driverless vehicles across the Mojave Desert and none finished. In 2005 five did, with Stanford's Stanley winning. The 2007 Urban Challenge moved the contest to a mock city with traffic, won by Carnegie Mellon's Boss.

**Why it matters:** They showed how quickly machine learning, sensors and software could solve a hard real-world problem when the goal and the prize were clear.

**Subfield:** Robotics / Machine Learning

**Impact:** Alumni of these teams helped start the self-driving programmes at major technology companies. Their work lives on in driver-assistance features such as adaptive cruise control and lane keeping, and in robotaxi services.

*Note on accuracy: Three separate events are shown as one entry, which is why the date is a range.*

## 11. The Netflix Prize (2006–2009)

**What happened:** Netflix released a large set of anonymised movie ratings and offered $1 million to any team that could improve its recommendation accuracy by 10 percent. Thousands of teams competed, and the prize was won in 2009 by a team of merged rivals.

**Why it matters:** It proved the value of open competitions, large datasets and ensembles of models, and it popularised collaborative filtering.

**Subfield:** Machine Learning

**Impact:** Recommendation engines now shape what people watch, buy, read and listen to on streaming, shopping and music services.

*Note on accuracy: Netflix later said it did not deploy the full winning ensemble because the engineering cost outweighed the gain. The prize's lasting value was the research it triggered.*

## 12. ImageNet is introduced (2009)

**What happened:** Fei-Fei Li and colleagues presented ImageNet, a database that grew to millions of images sorted into thousands of everyday categories. It later powered an annual image-recognition contest.

**Why it matters:** It established that data at scale can matter as much as clever algorithms, and it gave researchers a common benchmark to compare progress.

**Subfield:** Computer Vision / Data

**Impact:** Models pretrained on ImageNet-style data underpin photo search, medical image analysis, retail visual search and the camera features in modern phones.

## 13. Watson wins at Jeopardy! (2011)

**What happened:** In February 2011 IBM's Watson defeated Ken Jennings and Brad Rutter on the quiz show Jeopardy!, answering natural-language clues by searching and weighing evidence from millions of documents.

**Why it matters:** It was a major demonstration of question answering and of combining many language-processing techniques in one system.

**Subfield:** Natural Language Processing

**Impact:** It brought question-answering AI to a mass audience and spurred enterprise search and decision-support tools. IBM's later push into healthcare proved harder than the quiz show, a reminder that demos do not always transfer to messy real settings.

## 14. The deep learning revolution (2010s)

**What happened:** In the early 2010s, large datasets and powerful graphics processors made it practical to train deep neural networks. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) began to excel at recognising images.

**Why it matters:** It ended decades in which hand-built features dominated. Instead, networks learned useful features directly from data.

**Subfield:** Deep Learning

**Impact:** Within a few years, speech recognition, photo tagging, face unlock and machine translation on everyday devices improved dramatically, and industries from healthcare imaging to agriculture adopted vision models.

*Note on accuracy: The 2010s are an era rather than an event, so this entry marks the shift as a whole.*

## 15. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) (2014)

**What happened:** Ian Goodfellow and colleagues introduced GANs: a generator creates fake examples while a discriminator tries to spot them, and each improves by competing with the other.

**Why it matters:** GANs showed that neural networks could create new, realistic content rather than only classify existing data, opening the field of modern generative AI.

**Subfield:** Generative AI

**Impact:** They enabled realistic image synthesis, photo enhancement and synthetic training data for industry. They also made deepfakes possible, raising new problems for media trust and online safety.

## 16. The Transformer architecture (2017)

**What happened:** Researchers at Google published 'Attention Is All You Need', introducing the Transformer, a network design built on an attention mechanism that lets a model weigh the most relevant words in a sentence at once.

**Why it matters:** Attention made models far better at understanding context and much easier to train in parallel on large amounts of text, which made today's very large language models possible.

**Subfield:** LLMs / Deep Learning

**Impact:** Within five years Transformers powered assistants that draft emails, write code, translate languages and summarise documents, reshaping knowledge work across industries.

## 17. ChatGPT brings generative AI to the mainstream (2022)

**What happened:** OpenAI released ChatGPT on 30 November 2022. Built on a GPT language model tuned to follow instructions and hold a conversation, it was followed by rivals such as Claude and Gemini that write, translate, summarise and chat.

**Why it matters:** It turned a research technology into an everyday tool almost overnight and made generative AI a topic for everyone, not just specialists.

**Subfield:** Generative AI / LLMs

**Impact:** Writing, coding, customer support, education and research workflows changed within months. Schools, employers and governments are still working out rules for responsible use.

*Note on accuracy: Usage figures reported at the time (for example, 100 million users within about two months) came from third-party estimates, so cite them cautiously.*

## 18. Artificial general intelligence and superintelligence (Future)

**What happened:** Artificial general intelligence (AGI) means a system able to learn and perform most intellectual tasks as well as a person. Superintelligence would go well beyond human ability. Neither exists today.

**Why it matters:** Even as a theoretical goal, AGI shapes research priorities, investment and arguments about AI safety and regulation, and experts disagree sharply about whether and when it could happen.

**Subfield:** Future / Speculative

**Impact:** Industries and governments are already planning for both opportunity and risk, from the future of work to rules on how powerful AI systems are tested and deployed. Daily life may change in ways nobody can yet predict.

*Note on accuracy: This is speculative. There is no agreed definition and no consensus on a timeline, so treat any date you see as opinion.*

## 19. Rosenblatt builds the perceptron (1958)

**What happened:** Frank Rosenblatt at Cornell introduced the perceptron, a single-layer artificial neural network that learned to sort inputs into categories by adjusting its weights from examples. A US Navy-funded machine, the Mark I Perceptron, was built to recognise simple images.

**Why it matters:** It was an early working demonstration of machine learning. In 1969 Minsky and Papert showed that single-layer perceptrons cannot learn some simple patterns, which cooled interest in neural networks until multi-layer training arrived.

**Subfield:** Neural Networks

**Impact:** Artificial neurons are the building blocks of every modern deep network, from the models that tag photos and transcribe speech on your phone to those that flag fraudulent card payments.

*Note on accuracy: Added to the brief's starter bank. It sits in the machine learning layer because of what it introduced, even though it predates the layers above it in time.*

## 20. AlexNet wins ImageNet by a wide margin (2012)

**What happened:** Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton entered AlexNet, a deep convolutional network trained on GPUs, in the 2012 ImageNet challenge. It cut the top-5 error rate to about 15 percent while the next best entry was above 26 percent.

**Why it matters:** The size of the gap ended the argument. Deep learning was clearly better than the older approaches for vision, and research and funding moved quickly in that direction.

**Subfield:** Computer Vision / Deep Learning

**Impact:** Technology companies rebuilt their image, speech and ad-ranking systems around deep networks, which is why phone cameras, photo apps and visual search improved so fast.

*Note on accuracy: Added to the brief's starter bank because it is the moment ImageNet and GPUs paid off.*

## 21. AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol (2016)

**What happened:** In March 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Go champion Lee Sedol 4 to 1 in Seoul. It combined deep neural networks, reinforcement learning from self-play and tree search, and played moves that surprised professional players.

**Why it matters:** Go has far too many positions for brute force, so the win showed that learned intuition could rival human expertise in a very complex setting.

**Subfield:** Reinforcement Learning / Game AI

**Impact:** The same family of methods was later applied to problems such as data-centre cooling, chip design and scientific discovery, and it renewed worldwide interest in AI.

*Note on accuracy: Added to the brief's starter bank to show deep learning combined with reinforcement learning.*

## 22. GPT-3 shows what scale can do (2020)

**What happened:** OpenAI released GPT-3, a Transformer language model with 175 billion parameters, described in the paper 'Language Models are Few-Shot Learners'. It could write, translate and answer questions after seeing only a handful of examples in its prompt.

**Why it matters:** It showed that simply scaling up model size and training data produced surprising new abilities, and it set the pattern for the large language models that followed.

**Subfield:** LLMs

**Impact:** A commercial API followed in 2020, and developers built writing aids, support bots and coding helpers on top of it. It was the step that put large language models into everyday products.

*Note on accuracy: Added to the brief's starter bank to bridge the Transformer (2017) and ChatGPT (2022).*

## 23. AlphaFold 2 predicts protein structures (2020)

**What happened:** At the CASP14 assessment in late 2020, DeepMind's AlphaFold 2 predicted the 3D shapes of many proteins with accuracy close to laboratory experiments. DeepMind and EMBL-EBI later released predicted structures for over 200 million proteins.

**Why it matters:** Knowing a protein's shape is key to understanding what it does, and measuring shapes in the lab is slow and expensive. AlphaFold showed AI can speed up basic science.

**Subfield:** Deep Learning / Scientific AI

**Impact:** Biologists and drug developers now use the freely available structures to study diseases and design medicines faster. In 2024 the Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognised the work of Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, shared with David Baker.

*Note on accuracy: Added to the brief's starter bank to show AI's impact beyond consumer technology.*
